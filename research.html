Research

My research asks how motion and collective behavior emerge from many interacting particles and cells: how simple local rules give rise to coordinated migration, self-organization, and phase transitions at the group level. I build computational models, grounded in statistical physics and tested against quantitative experiments, to connect local interaction rules to behavior at the population or tissue scale. The thread running through this work is migration, from single cells and clusters to active particles and polymers, and I am now extending it to how geometry and mechanics shape tissues during development.

1. Collective Cell Migration: Chemotaxis and the Chemorepulsion Paradox

My postdoctoral research focuses on understanding how multicellular clusters migrate in response to chemical gradients. Using particle-based computational models informed by experimental observations of malignant lymphocyte clusters, I study chemotactic instabilities where clusters exhibit shape deformations, rotation, and fragmentation depending on gradient strength and cluster size. A particularly striking aspect of this work is a counterintuitive behavior observed at high gradient strengths: while single cells exhibit chemorepulsion and move away from the gradient source, larger clusters display chemoattraction and migrate up the gradient. I investigated the physical mechanisms underlying this size-dependent reversal, showing how collective effects and intercellular interactions enable clusters to maintain forward migration even under conditions that repel individual cells. This behavior has direct implications for understanding cancer metastasis and immune cell trafficking.

In a follow-up bioRxiv preprint, Solving the Chemorepulsive Paradox, I traced this reversal to its molecular origin. Single cells repel from the gradient because high ligand concentration drives a feedback loop in CCR7 receptor trafficking, depleting surface receptors and flipping the cell's polarity. Cell-cell contacts within a cluster change the geometry: each cell has less exposed membrane relative to its volume, which raises the concentration needed to trigger that same feedback and keeps clustered cells chemoattractive. Using an agent based model calibrated on receptor trafficking kinetics, I showed that cluster fluidity, cells continuously swapping between the rim and the core, further stabilizes this collective behavior by preventing repulsive cells from building up at the leading edge.

2. Morphogenesis and Mechanics: Gut-Tube Formation (Future Direction)

Looking ahead, I want to understand how geometry and mechanics regulate self-organization across many cells, and turn that understanding into design principles for biomaterials, organoids, and engineered tissues. Inspired by embryogenesis-on-chip studies that recreate neural tube and gut tube formation in micropatterned stem cell cultures, I am building cell-based computational models that include adhesion, migration, cell-type specification, and spatial confinement to understand how physical constraints guide tissue folding.

Planned questions

  • How do adhesion, cell migration, and cell-type specification together drive tissue folding and tube formation?
  • How does spatial confinement and pattern geometry determine the shape and size of the resulting tissue?
  • How do the migration models developed for cell clusters extend to realistic tissue architectures, and how does the physical microenvironment shape collective cancer invasion?
  • Which physical parameters could become design principles for organoids and engineered tissues?

3. Active Matter: Motility, Transport, and Phase Separation

Underlying both themes above is the physics of self-propelled units. Active particles, swimmers, and polymers can be viewed as migration at different scales, and I study how their motion, interactions, and surroundings determine collective behavior and transport.

Phase separation of soft active particles

During my PhD, I investigated how particle softness fundamentally alters motility-induced phase separation (MIPS) in active Brownian particles. While hard active particles form dense, compact clusters, I discovered that soft particles create porous, interconnected networks that continuously reorganize. My work revealed that the interplay between elasticity and self-propulsion controls transitions between compact, fluid-like, and percolating states. Using Brownian dynamics simulations and statistical analysis, I identified a novel percolation transition in these systems and characterized it by comparing critical exponents with literature values, confirming its distinct universality class.

Active swimmers and polymers: chirality and confinement

In parallel, I develop models of active swimmers and active polymers (including follower chains) to understand how coordinated migration emerges in elongated and self-propelled systems. I am studying how chirality and geometric confinement, compared with free, unconfined configurations, change their transport and coordination, with relevance to biological filaments, swimming microorganisms, and robotic systems. This work is ongoing and not yet published.
